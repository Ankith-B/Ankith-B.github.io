Cure · Sickle cell disease · Computational hematology

How much of the blood-forming system must be edited to protect against sickle cell crises?

A transplant-calibrated model and the one measurement that would settle it. Red-cell enrichment fitted in one transplant cohort predicts donor chimerism and hemoglobin in others, and implies that about 80–87% of circulating red cells must be protected. The required edited stem-cell fraction then runs from about 22% to 73%, depending on one unmeasured property of edited red cells.

Cure A Disease · 22 November 2026

Scope. Cure A Disease presents stem-cell editing as a cure for sickle cell disease. It has not been tested in humans. This is a computational analysis of published data; no experiment was performed. It concerns HbSS sickle cell disease only; beta-thalassemia, HbS-beta-zero, HbS-beta-plus and HbSC were not modelled because the needed data were not found. It is a prediction for testing.

Abstract

Gene-edited stem cell therapies for sickle cell disease have produced crisis-free outcomes in nearly all evaluable patients (29 of 30 free of vaso-occlusive crises for at least 12 months with exa-cel,1 from 44 treated), but those trials used high editing levels, so they do not show how little editing suffices. That question matters for approaches that edit stem cells in the body without wiping out the marrow, where company-reported monkey data reach about 58% of stem cells.2 We used allogeneic transplant cohorts, in which protection is measured as a function of donor chimerism, to calibrate a red-cell enrichment model. A recipient red-cell lifetime fitted in one cohort3 gives an enrichment odds factor of 19 (5–73), assuming a 120-day donor lifetime. Without refitting, the model predicts hemoglobin S in three patients from another cohort4 within −2 to +7 points (using stated assumptions about two hemoglobin values) and donor red-cell fractions in a third study5 within or at the edge of its 90% intervals. Reported protective donor chimerism of 17–26%6 maps to 80–87% protected circulating red cells. If edited cells survive like donor cells and are all protected, about 22% of stem cells must be edited (5–95%: 17–26%). If edited cells carry a quarter of that survival advantage, 52%; one-tenth, 73%. Existing trials constrain this only weakly. The decisive missing measurement is the edited fraction in stem cells versus red cells in patients with the lowest editing.

1 Approach

Transplant studies report donor chimerism in marrow-derived cells, and red cells often show higher donor fractions because donor red cells outlive sickle cells.3,5,7 We model donor red-cell fraction r from donor myeloid fraction d by r/(1−r) = K·d/(1−d). K is set by the fitted recipient red-cell lifetime of 6.22 days (95% CI 1.22–29.21; from a nonlinear regression, not a direct measurement3) divided into an assumed donor lifetime of 120 days. Protection thresholds come from cohorts: symptoms recurred in patients with 11% and 17% donor chimerism before graft failure, and the lowest chimerism in an asymptomatic patient was 26%,6 consistent with at least 20% in the cohort used for calibration.3 For editing, κ is the survival advantage of an edited red cell relative to a donor red cell, and π the share of edited red cells with enough anti-sickling globin to be protected. Neither is measured.

2 Verification status of inputs

Verified in source text: fitted lifetime and CI, 20% conclusion, n=67 (calibration cohort); 26% threshold and the 11% and 17% recurrences (n=95 cohort); the three patients’ chimerism and HbS values; enrichment of 2.0-fold (red cells vs erythroid progenitors) and 1.5-fold; the four donor-nucleated vs donor-red-cell pairs; 29/30 crisis-free.

Assumed or unverified: the 120-day donor lifetime (not in the retrieved text); recipient pre-transplant HbS (assumed 90%, tested 80–95%); donor HbS (source gives about 44–45% for two donors; 38% also tested); the disease of each patient in the red-cell pairs (hemoglobinopathies, thalassemia major or SCD; per-patient not retrieved); exa-cel F-cells (≥93%), HbF (about 40%) and editing (≥70% blood, ≥80% marrow), which come from a regulatory briefing we could not independently parse.

Misattributed earlier and corrected: the HbS values for years 2–5 in the calibration cohort apply only to the three patients who relapsed, not to all 67. They were not used.

Corrected in this posting after an independent check of the sources: an earlier draft stated that all patients at or below 17% donor chimerism relapsed. The published source reports only two patients with recurrent symptoms before graft failure, at 11% and 17%, so the statement has been narrowed accordingly. The cohort size of the second study (95 patients) was also reported inconsistently across versions of that paper, and should be confirmed against its full text.

3 Held-out tests

The red-cell predictions depend on the assumed donor lifetime: at 60 days the 15% case is predicted at 0.63; at 120 days 0.77; at 200 days 0.85. The data therefore favour roughly 100–120 days, but constrain it weakly. The edited-fraction thresholds in Section 4 do not depend on it, because the enrichment factor cancels. Chimerism measures differ across the studies (myeloid, whole blood), and four to seven points per test limit statistical power.

4 What this implies for editing

At π = 0.9, protection is impossible even with full replacement in 25% of draws, because the red-cell fraction needed (up to about 0.9) exceeds the protected share. Real trial patients have 93–99.9% F-cells in reports we could not fully verify, which would place π near the upper end.

Existing trials constrain κ only from below. With 80% bone-marrow editing and π = 0.95, the model gives protection probabilities of 0.91, 0.87, 0.47 and 0.00 at κ = 0.5, 0.25, 0.1 and 0.05. Observed near-universal protection therefore implies κ of at least about 0.1–0.25, but not whether it is near 1.

A 58% stem-cell edit may or may not suffice. At 58%, protection is 0.63 if κ = 1 (π = 0.9) but 0.09 if κ = 0.25 and 0.00 at 0.1. The company-reported monkey figure2 is therefore informative only once κ is known.

Loss of edited stem cells is fatal to the margin. In one rhesus animal, editing at the exa-cel target fell from 81–85% to 5–16% while another stayed near 75%, with no selective advantage reported.8 At 10–16% edited, the model predicts no protection under any κ.

5 The measurement that would settle it

Patients treated with edited stem cells should have paired measurements of edited fraction in stem cells (marrow CD34+) and in red cells, together with HbF per cell and clinical course. These pairs, especially for the lowest-editing patients, identify κ and π directly. Public per-patient data do not exist; individual plots in the regulatory briefing are figures only and could not be digitised. We suggest a prospective analysis of trial data by sponsors or regulators.

6 Safety and equity

Regulatory review of exa-cel reported no off-target editing at about 5,000 nominated sites, but a variant-aware search flagged an off-target at a variant common in people of African ancestry (allele frequency 4.5%), with allele-specific indels and pericentric inversions reported; rearrangement frequencies were not retrieved.9,10 Because SCD predominantly affects people of African ancestry, variant-aware safety analysis should be required for any editing design, including in vivo approaches.

7 Limitations

The enrichment factor rests on one fitted lifetime with a wide interval and an assumed donor lifetime; held-out tests have three to four points each and use assumed hemoglobin values. The threshold is a population-level relation from mean cohort data, not per patient. Thresholds differ between cohorts: the 17–26% range used here concerns resolution of symptoms, whereas another cohort that analysed multilineage chimerism reported that correcting hemolysis required higher donor chimerism (about 50% or more).4 We did not reconcile this; if the higher figure is the relevant one for a given patient, the required edited fraction would be higher than reported here. κ and π are unidentified; polymerisation physics (delay time scaling with roughly the 30th power of HbS concentration; HbF hybrids largely excluded from polymer11) supports a strong per-cell protective effect but could not be turned into a calibrated per-cell model because the red-cell concentration distribution and exa-cel per-cell HbF data were not found. Transplant protection may differ from edited-cell protection (graft-versus-host, different hemolysis). Beta-thalassemia, other genotypes and alpha-thalassemia effects are not modelled. Several sources were read only as abstracts or summaries, and the reference list is abbreviated. Whether this question has been addressed elsewhere cannot be excluded; we did not find it in our searches.

8 Predictions

  1. In trial patients, edited fraction in red cells should exceed edited fraction in stem cells by an odds factor related to κ×19; the paired data would reveal it.
  2. No patient with under about 20% of stem cells edited should be protected if κ is near 1; under 50% if κ is near 0.25.
  3. In vivo editing at about 58% of stem cells will protect most patients only if κ is at least about 0.5.
  4. Partial or lost response should appear first in patients with the lowest edited stem-cell fraction at engraftment, and in those whose edited fraction drifts down over time.
