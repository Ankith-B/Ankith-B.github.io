Cure · Wilson disease · Computational hepatology · Version 3

What fraction of ATP7B must be restored, and in which patients?

An evidence-graded model of correction, copper handling and outcome in Wilson disease. Required correction depends mainly on genotype: about 21% of alleles in H1069Q homozygotes against 54% with a null partner allele. Independent animal data partly conflict with the human-derived threshold, and no dataset yet validates the correction-to-rescue relationship.

Cure A Disease · 22 November 2026 · Supersedes versions 1 and 2

Status. Computational analysis of published data. No experiment was performed. Cure A Disease presents correction of ATP7B as a cure for Wilson disease. It has not been tested in humans. This document states which claims are measured, which are modelled, and which are assumed.

Abstract

We extended a hepatic copper-store model into a chain from allele correction through protein activity, copper excretion, liver injury, blood markers and brain scenarios, applied it to five genotype classes, and audited it against newly extracted data. A fact-check of version 2 corrected several overstatements (Section 2). The human-derived hepatic-injury threshold is 0.30 of normal capacity (5–95%: 0.14–0.43). Required per-allele correction is 21% (5–95%: 5–37%) in H1069Q homozygotes, 31% with a residual-function missense partner, 49% with a severe missense partner and 54% (18–84%) with a null partner. At 50% correction, success probability is 1.00, 0.85, 0.53 and 0.42 respectively. Corrected-protein activity of 50% cuts the null-partner probability to 0.07; 40% irreversible liver loss to 0.09. Held-out tests give a mixed verdict: carrier-donor liver copper is predicted (111–183 vs 147–159 µg/g, n=2), but the model assigns only 0.15 probability to the mouse finding that 20% biallelic correction suffices, and over-predicts late copper accumulation in untreated rats by 26%. Brain copper cannot be quantified from available data. The result is a prioritised, falsifiable prediction, not validation.

Where this happens

ATP7B works inside liver cells, loading excess copper into bile for removal. When it fails, copper builds up in the liver. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom, and use the checkboxes to isolate structures.

Why this is a cure

Wilson disease is caused by loss of ATP7B, the liver protein that moves copper into bile. Chelators and zinc manage the copper but must be taken for life and do not repair the gene. Correcting ATP7B in enough hepatocytes restores the excretion route itself, so copper no longer accumulates and the drugs are not needed. That is a cure in the plain sense: the cause is fixed rather than the symptoms held back. The question this paper answers is how much correction counts. The answer is a number per genotype (21% of alleles in H1069Q homozygotes up to 54% with a null partner allele), and in the model, at 50% correction a H1069Q homozygote reaches sufficiency with probability 1.00. The nearest human evidence supports the idea that a carrier-level liver is enough: after liver transplant from carrier donors, 24 of 32 recipients were alive without recurrence at up to 15 years, and the model predicts donor-liver copper within the observed range. Section 2 states what that evidence does not show.

1 Evidence base and what was held out

Automated literature searches, four in this version and four earlier, collected numeric data; values were kept only when read in a source, and figure-only or unread values are labelled missing. Used to calibrate: hepatic copper in untreated patients (771 ± 393 µg/g, n=178) and heterozygotes (111 ± 52, n=24);1 cohort biomarker means for controls, heterozygotes and patients;2 H1069Q copper uptake about 10% of wild type in insect-cell microsomes;3 and genotype-class activities for several missense variants.3 Held out for testing: carrier-donor liver copper (147, 159 µg/g, n=2);4 LEC rat hepatic copper time courses across treatments;5,6 the 20% transduction result in Atp7b-null mice;7 genotype severity tiers (onset 13, 16, 22 years; ceruloplasmin oxidase 0, 3.1, 17.8 U/L);8 and a human-cell engraftment study.9 Most dose-response values in the animal literature are in figures or supplements and could not be extracted.

2 Fact-check: corrections to earlier versions

Carrier-liver transplant is not a clean “cure” bound. Of 32 recipients in the largest series, 24 were alive without recurrence at up to 15 years, 6 died and 2 were retransplanted; another series followed patients for only 2–27 months. Urinary copper stayed above normal (74 µg/day). The realistic endpoint is heterozygote-like, not normal, and the “58 recipients, no recurrence” framing overstated it.

250 µg/g is a diagnostic cutoff, not an injury tolerance. The best cutoff in the source is 209 µg/g; 4 of 24 heterozygotes were at or above 150 and one above 250. The injury-copper prior is widened to 150–300.

Three different thresholds were conflated. 20% (mouse transduction), 25% (a stated premise, not a finding) and 20–30% (an uncited company statement) are non-equivalent, and hepatocyte fraction is not ATP7B function fraction.

Smaller points. Half-life values for H1069Q (1.5 h vs 8 h) could not be retrieved; the abstract reports only a five-fold decrease. VTX-801 is recorded as stopped for futility with no responders, but the registry status reads active, not recruiting. All other checked numbers (cohort biomarkers, hepatic copper, trial design, genotype counts, rat copper, uptake data) were verified against source text.

Genotype mix depends on the cohort. One cohort had 74% compound heterozygotes; a German series had H1069Q homozygosity in 51% and compound heterozygosity in 25% of patients. Results are therefore reported by genotype, not pooled.

3 Model chain

(i) Correction: per-allele rate a. (ii) Protein and trafficking: a corrected allele has activity g (1 = wild type). Uncorrected alleles keep class-specific residual activity from published ratios: H1069Q about 0.10 (alternatively 0.10–0.50 from an overexpression assay that disagrees), null 0, severe missense 0–0.10, residual-function missense 0.15–0.30, R778L 0–0.30 (value missing, so wide). (iii) Capacity is the mean allele activity, then scaled by (1 − irreversible liver loss). (iv) Copper store: power-law excretion calibrated to heterozygote copper, giving the threshold. (v) Dynamics dm/dt = k(1 − c·mn) with k set from an assumed age at diagnosis of 15 years. (vi) Injury: reversible injury accumulates when copper exceeds the cutoff and recovers; irreversible loss is an input, not a prediction. (vii) Blood markers interpolate three cohort means across capacity. (viii) Brain: only a clearance scenario with a 2- or 10-year half-time, illustrative because no quantitative brain copper or recovery data were retrievable.

4 Results

Genotype dominates. A null partner allele roughly doubles the required correction compared with homozygosity.

Incomplete correction and protein activity. If a corrected allele delivers only half the activity (low expression or mislocalisation), success at 50% correction falls from 1.00 to 0.53 in homozygotes and from 0.42 to 0.07 with a null partner.

Loss of corrected cells. A 10% yearly loss leaves 30% correction after five years, with success 0.83 and 0.14. A 19% yearly dilution, as for non-integrating episomes under hepatocyte turnover, gives 0.37 and 0.05. Genomic edits are not diluted this way.

Established damage. If 40% of functional liver mass is irreversibly lost, success falls to 0.65 and 0.09. This is an assumed scenario; no fibrosis-regression data were retrievable.

Timescale. In homozygotes at 50% correction, hepatic copper falls below 209 µg/g after about 6 years; with a null partner at 70% correction, about 15 years; at 50%, not within 20 years. Absolute times are uncertain by at least a factor of two because the model failed the held-out accumulation check. (The predicted copper-decline curves are not plotted here because their underlying values were not part of this document.)

Unintended edits. Exposure scales with edited cells: at 50% correction of about 1.5×1011 hepatocytes, a per-cell off-target rate of 10−4 would mean about 107 affected cells. No measured rate exists for this construct, so this is illustrative.

5 Independent tests

Two tests agree, two conflict, two are weak. The most important conflict is that mouse data suggest a lower threshold than the human-derived one. The mouse and rat models differ from human physiology and from each other, and the model transferred its human-calibrated exponent to them, so the conflict does not show which is wrong; it shows the threshold is uncertain between about 0.1 and 0.4. The rat dose-response is ordinal because repopulation fraction was not measured, so no dataset tests the correction-to-capacity step directly.

6 Human-cell correction evidence

A 2026 study corrected H1069Q in patient-derived iPSCs and reported restored ATP7B trafficking and high copper resistance in hepatocyte-like cells.10 Only the abstract was accessible (full text paywalled; an erratum exists), so efficiency, zygosity, off-target results and assay values are not extracted. An R778L study obtained one heterozygous corrected clone, found no indels at ten predicted sites, and reported improved hepatic copper, enzymes and fibrosis in mice at about 5% engraftment (figure-only).9 An engineered-cell study reported 0% survival of knockout cells against 46–48% of repaired clones at 0.6 mM copper.11 These support two premises: single-allele correction restores relevant cell function, and low engraftment can help. They provide no graded threshold. No peer-reviewed in vivo ATP7B editing paper was found; sponsor figures (up to 80% correction in a humanized mouse, 51% in monkeys with a surrogate editor) are company-reported.

7 Off-target and durability

No ATP7B-specific off-target dataset was found. Liver prime editing in PKU mice showed no detectable off-target mutations,12 and LNP-delivered editing has been durable for at least 8 months in monkeys13 and a year in humans (TTR reduced about 90%).14 These support feasibility, not safety of this construct.

8 What would make this a cure, and what this does not show

A cure would require treated patients to remain biochemically and clinically controlled off standard therapy for years, with stable neurological status. None of that has been measured. This work narrows what correction level and genotype to look for. What it cannot establish: that correction restores full protein function in patients, that established fibrosis or brain damage reverses, that off-target effects are acceptable, or that results hold across populations. The ongoing Phase 1/2 trial (about 42 patients; primary endpoint adverse events; results not yet posted) is the first test.

9 Predictions to check against trial data

  1. Homozygotes should respond at lower editing than null-partner compound heterozygotes; the latter near 30% correction should not normalise exchangeable copper.
  2. Hepatic copper should change little (within about 10%) at 48 weeks; a decline over years is expected.
  3. A corrected protein with reduced activity would show up as a plateau in ceruloplasmin below the carrier level despite high editing.
  4. Neurological change should lag hepatic and blood markers; no timescale can be predicted from current data.

10 Limitations

Calibration rests on one heterozygote copper mean and an assumed excretion form; normal and injury copper are assumptions. Blood-marker predictions interpolate three cohort means from different studies. Residual activities for several variants come from one insect-cell assay that disagrees with an overexpression study, and the R778L value is missing. Injury, fibrosis and brain components are scenarios, not fitted models. Many dose-response values were figure-only or behind paywalls; some sources were read only as abstracts, and the reference list is abbreviated and was machine-retrieved. Human-derived and animal-derived thresholds conflict and are not reconciled.

Where correction stands today

Everything here comes from search summaries of company releases, registries and abstracts, not from the full papers, and none of it is peer-reviewed human efficacy data. It is context for the cure claim, not proof of it.

  • AAV gene addition in people. VTX-801 (Vivet) is in the phase 1/2 GATEWAY trial (NCT04537377, 4 patients enrolled). Company-reported interim data in 2 patients showed ceruloplasmin ferroxidase activity rising, no serious adverse events, and normalized fibrosis and iron scores on year-1 biopsy. Earlier in this paper the same program is recorded as stopped for futility with no responders; those two reports conflict and I have not resolved them. UX701 (Ultragenyx, AAV9) enrolled 15 patients in three dose cohorts with at least 24 weeks of follow-up and reported “meaningful clinical activity”; a fourth cohort was added at a somewhat higher dose.
  • mRNA in lipid nanoparticles. A 2025 paper reports a liver-targeted nanoparticle carrying full-length ATP7B mRNA reduced copper overload and restored ceruloplasmin activity in a Wilson disease mouse model. A first-in-human mRNA trial (DSL101, NCT07240896) is registered at phase 1 with no results.
  • Gene editing. A company poster reports up to 80% correction of H1069Q in a humanized mouse with a prime editor and a 75% fall in liver copper. This is not peer-reviewed. I found no base-editing study of H1069Q. A JCI Insight paper estimates that ATP7B function in at least 25% of hepatocytes rescues copper handling in mice, which is close to this model’s own thresholds.
  • Carriers are healthy. Heterozygotes are generally asymptomatic with one working allele, which is why partial correction is plausible. This is an inference from carrier health and the 25% mouse estimate, not a measured human threshold.
  • Transplant as the existing cure. In US registry data, adult survival after liver transplant for Wilson disease was about 87.5%, 85.4% and 80.5% at 3, 5 and 10 years; paediatric survival was 90.5%, 89.7% and 86.5%. A French cohort of 121 patients reported 87% survival at 5, 10 and 15 years. Neurologic outcome varies. Transplant is a real cure of the liver defect but costs a major operation and, in general, lifelong immunosuppression, which correction of a patient’s own liver cells would avoid.
