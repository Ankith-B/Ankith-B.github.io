Cure · High cholesterol and heart disease · Computational cardiology

A one-time cure for high LDL cholesterol: how much of the liver must be edited, and what it does to heart attacks over decades

Switching off PCSK9 in the liver once is predicted to lower LDL cholesterol for life. Editing about half of the liver (32–67% allowing for model error) is predicted to halve LDL-C. The predicted drop in cardiovascular events then grows with the years of exposure: about 38% over five years and about 75% after thirty, if the effect lasts.

Cure A Disease · 23 November 2026

Scope. Cure A Disease presents one-time PCSK9 liver editing as a cure for high LDL cholesterol and the heart disease it drives. This is a computational analysis of published data; no experiment was performed. Unlike the other papers on this site, the editing has been given to people: a 2026 trial in 35 patients measured LDL-C, not heart attacks or strokes. Every event figure below is a model extrapolation, not an observed result, and safety is not modelled. It is a prediction for testing, not evidence that any therapy prevents disease.

Abstract

High LDL cholesterol is the main modifiable cause of heart attacks and strokes, and today it is managed with daily or periodic drugs for life. A cure would remove the cause once. In the VERVE-102 phase 1 trial, a single infusion of a liver-targeted base editor that disables PCSK9 lowered PCSK9 by 51% to 88% and LDL-C by 9% to 62% across doses in 35 patients.1 Two questions remain: how much of the liver has to be edited, and what the lowering would do to events over decades. We chain three calibrated steps: liver editing to PCSK9 reduction, PCSK9 reduction to LDL-C reduction, and LDL-C reduction to event risk, with a term for how benefit grows with years of exposure anchored on trial,2 genetic3 and cumulative-exposure4 evidence. Halving LDL-C needs a median 49% bulk-liver editing (5–95%: 32–67%). For a person with LDL-C 3.5 mmol/L and a 55% reduction, relative event risk is predicted at 0.62 after 5 years, 0.35 after 20 and about 0.25 after 30, against a constant 0.62 if the short-term trial effect were simply extended. The PCSK9-to-LDL step fitted in humans transferred poorly to mouse and primate data (one of five held-out points inside its interval), which widens the editing estimates. The decisive missing data are paired liver editing fractions and LDL-C in treated patients.

Why this is a cure

The cause of the disease here is a lifetime of excess LDL particles in the blood, controlled by how many LDL receptors the liver displays. PCSK9 destroys those receptors. People born with a disabled PCSK9 gene have lifelong low LDL and, in the ARIC cohort, an 88% lower risk of coronary heart disease for nonsense-variant carriers and 47% lower for a milder R46L variant,7 with no sign of illness from having the gene switched off. A one-time edit copies that natural experiment: it permanently changes what the liver does, so no daily pill or periodic injection is needed. That is a cure of the lipid disorder in the plain sense, and the model asks whether it also cures the disease the lipids cause. Heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, with lifelong high LDL from birth, is the clearest case. What is shown in humans is the LDL lowering; what is predicted is the event benefit.

1 Approach

Step 1, editing to knockdown (calibrated). PCSK9 falls by k = min(1, φf), where f is the fraction of liver alleles edited. Three paired animal values (macaque, 26% edited and PCSK9 −32%; mouse, 61% and −95%; primate, 66% and −90%) give φ = 1.23, 1.56 and 1.36, median 1.38. The factor above 1 is consistent with bulk liver containing unedited non-liver cells.5,6 Step 2, knockdown to LDL-C (calibrated). LDL-C falls by L = Lmax·kp, fitted to five human VERVE-102 points: Lmax 0.83 (0.72–0.90), p 1.35 (0.9–1.75), RMSE 0.14. Step 3, LDL-C to events (modelled). Log relative risk is −ΔL·β(T) for T years of lowering by ΔL mmol/L. β starts at the trial value 0.248 per mmol/L (rate ratio 0.78, 95% CI 0.76–0.80, about 5 years of follow-up2), rises by γ = 0.020 per mmol/L-year (hazard ratio 1.053 per 100 mg/dL-years4) beyond year 5, and is capped at the lifelong genetic value 0.787 (54.5% lower risk per mmol/L, CI 48.8–59.53). The cap is reached after about 32 years. That the three anchors combine this way is an assumption.

Arithmetic checks on these inputs: −ln 0.78 = 0.248; −ln(1 − 0.545) = 0.788; ln 1.053 per 100 mg/dL-years is 0.0516, which is 0.020 per mmol/L-year using 100 mg/dL = 2.59 mmol/L; and a 50% LDL-C reduction needs k = 0.69 and so f = 0.69/1.38 = 50%, matching the stated 49%.

2 Verification status of inputs

Checked against source records for this posting: VERVE-102 (N Engl J Med 2026, doi 10.1056/NEJMoa2601283): 35 patients, PCSK9 −51% to −88%, LDL-C −9% to −62%, no dose-limiting toxicity, follow-up of at least a year in 15 patients and up to 18 months; Ference 2012: 54.5% per mmol/L (48.8–59.5%), n = 312,321; Cohen 2006: 88% lower coronary risk with 28% lower LDL-C for nonsense carriers, 47% (HR 0.50) with 15% lower LDL-C for R46L; Domanski 2020: HR 1.053 per 100 mg/dL-years in CARDIA; Ference 2016: 0.81 (0.74–0.89) per 10 mg/dL for the PCSK9 genetic score. Standard but not re-checked: the Cholesterol Treatment Trialists’ rate ratio of 0.78 (0.76–0.80) per mmol/L. Not verified: the PubMed identifier originally listed for VERVE-102 (the DOI is cited instead); the Raal hazard ratio of 0.34 used in the familial hypercholesterolemia check; the per-dose breakdowns and press-release cohorts; the primate and mouse editing values; and the 66% primate editing value, which came from a table snippet. No human liver editing fraction has been reported.

3 Held-out tests

The held-out RMSE is 0.11 of LDL-C, so step 2 does not transfer reliably across species or compounds. That error is added to the editing estimates below. For step 3, predicted relative coronary risk for the PCSK9 nonsense carriers of Cohen et al.7 is 0.48 (0.42–0.56) against 0.11 observed (CI 0.02–0.81), and 0.67 (0.62–0.72) against 0.50 (0.32–0.79) for R46L carriers. Both fall inside the observed intervals, which are wide, and both depend on assumed baseline LDL-C. The prediction of 0.816 per 10 mg/dL against the observed 0.81 (0.74–0.89)8 is not independent, since it uses the same genetic framework. A check in homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia fails at a 25-year horizon (predicted 0.07, observed 0.34, CI 0.14–0.86;9 0.15 at 15 years is inside). The design of that study is not known precisely, so it is read as a limit on extrapolation to LDL-C reductions above about 3 mmol/L, not a clean test.

4 What this implies

The main implication is about time. A 55% LDL-C reduction at 3.5 mmol/L is predicted to prevent about 38% of events in a five-year window but about 75% of events after three decades of lowering. The first number resembles what drug trials show; the second is the long-exposure effect seen in genetic studies, reached only if the lowering persists. Persistence is the property a one-time therapy has that a daily pill does not, and it is also the property no human data yet test beyond about a year and a half.1 Baseline LDL-C is the strongest determinant: the absolute lowering, not the percentage, drives the effect.

5 The measurement that would settle it

No trial has reported a liver editing fraction; human editing is inferred only from blood PCSK9. Paired liver biopsy editing, PCSK9 and LDL-C in a few patients would fix steps 1 and 2 in humans and replace the animal-derived φ and the cross-species error. For step 3, imaging endpoints such as coronary plaque progression over several years would test the duration term directly, long before event trials could.

6 Safety

Efficacy and safety must be read together. In the first-generation program, a patient with severe coronary disease died of cardiac arrest about five weeks after infusion at 0.3 mg/kg, a myocardial infarction occurred the day after infusion at 0.45 mg/kg, and grade 3 transaminase elevation with grade 3 thrombocytopenia attributed to the lipid nanoparticle paused enrollment.10 The second-generation trial reported no dose-limiting toxicity, with mild-to-moderate infusion reactions and transient liver-enzyme rises.1 Off-target editing was not found in a primate study,5 but human follow-up is short, and re-dosing did not raise editing in primates. The long-term effects of permanently switching off PCSK9 are known only from carriers of natural variants. No risk-benefit estimate is made here.

7 Limitations

Step 2 rests on five human points from an abstract and press releases and failed four of five held-out checks. φ rests on three paired values, one unverified. The duration term is an assumed interpolation between a trial rate ratio and a genetic estimate, which may be inflated by lifelong exposure, pleiotropy or LDL-C measured once; γ comes from a composite outcome in a young cohort without a reported interval, and 20% uncertainty was assigned by assumption. Predictions assume permanent editing and no drift in the edited fraction, on which no data bear. Absolute risk was not computed. The model is valid only for LDL-C reductions below about 3 mmol/L.

8 Predictions

  • Patients with a 50% LDL-C reduction should show bulk-liver editing of about 32–67%; a value outside this range falsifies steps 1 and 2.
  • Coronary plaque progression in treated patients should slow in proportion to the absolute LDL-C reduction and keep diverging from statin-trial slopes over years.
  • At a PCSK9 reduction of 90%, the human LDL-C response should stay near 0.73 of baseline or below the animal values of 0.58–0.60; a larger human response would mean the animal mapping is conservative.
  • If editing wanes, risk should follow the shorter-horizon rows of Table 3, not the lifelong row.
