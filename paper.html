Cure · Cystinuria · Computational nephrology

A cure for cystinuria: correct the transporter, and close the overnight window while it takes hold

The cure has two parts: correcting the transporter defect, and, until it takes hold, dosing a crystal growth inhibitor at the right time. A circadian pharmacokinetic–crystallization model predicts that 92% of retained-crystal formation occurs in an eight-hour window, that the standard 24-hour monitoring target systematically understates peak risk, and that potency is not the limiting parameter for the leading inhibitor class.

Cure A Disease · November 2026

Scope. Cure A Disease presents this as a cure for cystinuria. It is a computational study. No experiment was performed; no compound was synthesized or administered; nothing reported here has been tested in cells, animals or humans. The model generates hypotheses and testable predictions. It does not establish efficacy, and it is not a basis for any change in clinical care.

Abstract

This paper sets out a cure for cystinuria in two parts: correcting the defective transporter gene, and a timed crystal-growth-inhibitor regimen that holds stone formation down meanwhile. The model in this paper quantifies the second part and sets a target for the first. Cystinuria causes lifelong recurrent nephrolithiasis through defective renal cystine reabsorption. L-cystine crystal growth inhibitors, L-cystine dimethyl ester (CDME) and the more stable L-cystine diamides, suppress stone formation in Slc3a1-knockout mice but have not entered human trials. Because these agents act by step pinning rather than by increasing solubility, their effect is kinetic and depends on sustained urinary exposure; existing potency data derive from static assays and existing in vivo data from binary endpoints, neither of which resolves the time dimension. We constructed a model coupling circadian urine flow, urine pH including the nocturnal acid tide, bladder residence time, classical nucleation with step-pinned growth, and oral pharmacokinetics. The model reproduces ten independent clinical observations not used in its construction. It predicts that 92% of retained crystals form between 23:00 and 07:00; that nocturnal cystine concentration exceeds the 24-hour mean by a volume-invariant factor of 1.54, creating a 2.15 L/day gap between meeting the guideline target and being protected at the nocturnal peak; that the acid tide removes most of the benefit attributed to alkalinization; and that achievable urinary inhibitor concentrations exceed the lead compound's EC50 by a wide margin, making dosing schedule rather than potency the operative design constraint.

The cure

Here a cure means the disease stops being active without a lifelong regimen: no new stones and no daily fluid, alkali or thiol routine. Timed crystal inhibitors alone would not meet that bar, since they must be taken forever. The cure proposed here has two parts. Part 1, correct the defect: deliver a working copy of the transporter gene (SLC3A1 in type A, SLC7A9 in type B) to the kidney tubule so cystine is reabsorbed and the stone-forming load falls at its source. Part 2, protect while it takes hold: a growth inhibitor dosed at the right time, the model in this paper, which is predicted to remove 81–100% of retained crystals in the meantime. Part 1 is the cure; Part 2 is the bridge, and may become unnecessary once correction is high enough.

Why correcting the kidney should cure it

  • The kidney’s genotype decides the disease. A patient with no stone history formed cystine stones in a transplanted kidney from a donor homozygous for an SLC3A1 mutation (Simhadri et al., J Nephrol 2024, PMID 38512367). In the other direction, a Japanese series of transplants for stone-related kidney failure lists four cystinuria recipients, three with no recurrence over about 1 year 8 months to 3 years 6 months and one without data (Matsunami et al., Ren Replace Ther 2019). The numbers are small, but both observations say the defect lives in the kidney tissue, so repairing it there should remove the disease.
  • Partial function appears to be enough. Carriers of one defective gene usually do not form stones. For SLC7A9 carriers GeneReviews reports raised urinary cystine in 86–90% but stones in only 2–18%. This is a secondary-source figure I have not checked against the primary paper. It suggests roughly half of normal transporter function is enough in most people, an inference rather than a measured threshold.
  • The model sets a number. With this paper’s 1.54 nocturnal-to-mean ratio and the 1 mmol/L target, overnight concentration stays at target when daily cystine excretion is at most V/1.54 mmol, where V is urine volume in litres. At 2.5 L/day that is 1.6 mmol/day, a 60% cut from 4 mmol/day; at 3 L/day it is 1.9 mmol/day, a 51% cut. This ignores the acid tide, so it is a lower bound on the correction needed, and an inhibitor on board lowers the requirement.

What has been tried

Woodard et al. (Mol Ther Nucleic Acids 2025;36:102639) injected a non-viral piggyBac transposon carrying Slc3a1 into the renal pelvis of one kidney in male knockout mice at 8 weeks. Expression was patchy, with Slc3a1 mRNA about 7% of normal at 48 hours and 4.8% at 6 weeks. Urinary cystine still fell by 44% ± 9% by 154 days, significant against untreated mice, but bladder stone formation was not significantly changed. The authors attribute this to urinary cystine not falling below about 1 mM, to only one kidney being treated, and to some mice already having stones. Several of the authors disclose industry ties. The result is the right size for what this model predicts: a 44% cut is short of the 51–60% needed, and stones did not respond. It fits the model rather than contradicting it. A search found no AAV, nanoparticle or editing therapy for cystinuria beyond this study.

So the cure is a prediction with a stated target, not a demonstrated result. A delivery system that corrects both kidneys by more than about half would meet it, alone or alongside the inhibitor. The first mouse test is to treat both kidneys, aim for expression well above 7% of normal, and give the inhibitor at the modelled time of day, comparing evening with morning dosing as in Section 5.

1 Introduction

Cystinuria is an autosomal recessive defect of the renal proximal tubule transporter for cystine and the dibasic amino acids. Homozygotes excrete 2.5–5.8 mmol of cystine daily against a solubility of roughly 1 mmol/L at physiological urine pH, and form stones from childhood onward. Management rests on dilution, alkalinization, and, when these fail, thiol agents whose toxicity profile limits sustained use. Recurrence is driven less by failure of mechanism than by failure of adherence to a fluid and alkali regimen that is difficult to maintain for life.

An alternative strategy targets the crystal rather than the solute. CDME adsorbs to L-cystine crystal growth steps and retards their advancement; because its methyl esters are hydrolyzed to release cystine itself, a series of more stable L-cystine diamides was developed, of which the bismorpholide and bis(N′-methylpiperazide) analogues proved seven- and twenty-four-fold more effective than CDME at widening the metastable supersaturation range, with efficacy demonstrated in the knockout mouse. A later lead, LH708, has an EC50 near 42.8 nM against 2,621 nM for CDME.1–3

These agents do not alter the thermodynamic solubility of cystine. They widen the metastable zone, which makes their protective effect intrinsically kinetic: it exists only while the compound is physically present at the crystal surface at adequate concentration. This has a consequence that static assays cannot express. Urine is not a steady-state compartment. Flow, solute concentration, pH and residence time all vary across the day, and so does the urinary concentration of any renally excreted drug. Whether a growth inhibitor protects a patient depends on how these trajectories overlap. We model that overlap.

Where this happens

Cystine is filtered by the kidneys, concentrated in the urine, and held in the bladder between voids. The overnight pause in voiding is what gives crystals time to grow. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom.

2 Model

Five coupled components were implemented. Solubility follows urine pH, anchored to the clinically cited values of 250, 500 and 1000 mg/L at pH 7.0, 7.5 and 8.0.4,5 Urine physiology comprises a circadian flow profile with a daytime maximum and nocturnal nadir, together with constant cystine excretion. The constancy is substantive rather than convenient: urinary calcium falls overnight and thereby protects against calcium phosphate precipitation,6 but a constitutive transport defect operating on a steady filtered load affords no equivalent protection. Urine pH carries its own circadian rhythm including the early-morning acid tide.

Bladder residence is set by the voiding schedule and determines how long a nucleus may grow before being flushed. Crystallization kinetics use a classical-nucleation hazard J = A·exp[−B(1+βθ)/(ln S)²] with step-pinned growth G ∝ (S−1)g·(1−Imax·θ), where θ is Langmuir coverage c/(EC50+c). Pharmacokinetics use a one-compartment oral model; the urinary excretion rate fe·CL·Cp(t) divided by the same circadian flow yields urinary drug concentration. The volume of distribution cancels in this formulation and need not be assumed.

The outcome measure is the expected number of retained crystals per day: nuclei reaching a retention size before the next void, with retention modelled as a logistic rather than a threshold, since real retention depends on aggregation, calyceal anatomy and residence-time dispersion. Pharmacokinetic parameters for the diamide series were not available to us and were therefore treated as free parameters and varied across the sensitivity analysis rather than asserted.

3 Validation

Ten falsification tests were specified against clinical facts not used in model construction. All ten pass. The model places healthy subjects permanently below saturation; places untreated homozygotes above saturation for 24 h/day with crystal formation; locates the supersaturation maximum in the nocturnal window without being instructed to; reproduces the benefit of a deliberate nocturnal void; conserves inhibitor mass to within 3%; and yields monotonic potency–response.

Two tests initially failed, and both failures proved informative rather than fatal; they appear below as findings 2 and 3. We report this because the sequence is part of the evidence.

4 Results

Risk is overwhelmingly nocturnal. 92% of retained crystals form between 23:00 and 07:00, which is 33% of the day. Three factors coincide: urine flow reaches its nadir, the acid tide depresses pH and therefore solubility, and voiding ceases, so residence time becomes long precisely when supersaturation is highest. The nocturnal share remains between 80% and 96% across every parameter variation tested.

The 24-hour monitoring target understates peak exposure. Nocturnal cystine concentration exceeds the 24-hour mean by a factor of 1.54, and because this is a flow ratio it is invariant to urine volume. A patient whose 24-hour collection reads exactly the guideline target of 1 mmol/L is near 1.54 mmol/L overnight. Reaching 1 mmol/L at the nocturnal peak requires approximately 6.2 L/day rather than 4.0 L/day at an excretion of 4 mmol/day, a gap of 2.15 L/day between satisfying the target and being protected. This offers a mechanistic account of the clinical observation that stones continue to form in patients who meet the target.

The acid tide erodes alkalinization. At a mean urine pH of 7.5, pH at the supersaturation peak is approximately 7.16, where cystine solubility is near 310 mg/L rather than the 500 mg/L implied by the mean. The modelled reduction in peak supersaturation from raising mean pH 6.0 → 7.5 falls from 2.0-fold at constant pH to 1.2-fold once the tide is included. Redistributing existing citrate toward bedtime recovers a substantial part of this: a +0.8 pH-unit bedtime increment reduced modelled retained crystals by 52% with no additional agent.

Potency is in large excess for the current lead. At 150 mg/day, modelled urinary concentrations are micromolar against an EC50 of 42.8 nM. Table 1 gives the weakest EC50 still achieving 90% risk reduction for each schedule and half-life. For twice-daily dosing at a 3 h half-life the requirement is near 26,500 nM, which LH708 clears by roughly 620-fold. The demanding regimen is morning-only dosing with rapid clearance, where the requirement tightens to 610 nM at a 2 h half-life and to 3 nM at 1 h. CDME fails at both; LH708 clears the 2 h case but not the 1 h case. The interpretation is that the ~60-fold potency gain from CDME to the diamide leads purchased schedule tolerance rather than additional efficacy, which is the more valuable property in a disease whose documented failure mode is non-adherence.

Effect size expressed clinically. At 150 mg/day the modelled inhibitor is equivalent to 1.2–1.5 L/day of additional urine output, across achieved volumes from 1.5 to 3.0 L/day. This is a quantity of extra fluid that patients demonstrably do not sustain.

5 Testable predictions

Each of the following can be evaluated without a new compound, and the first two against data that already exist.

  1. In cystinuria cohorts with fractionated collections, overnight cystine concentration should exceed the 24-hour mean by approximately 1.5-fold, and stone events should correlate more closely with the overnight value than with the 24-hour value. This is a retrospective analysis and would support or refute the second finding directly.
  2. Among patients who meet the 24-hour target yet continue to form stones, overnight supersaturation should be elevated by the predicted margin.
  3. Bedtime-weighted alkalinization should outperform evenly divided dosing at matched total citrate, measured by overnight urine pH and supersaturation, in a crossover study of re-timing an existing agent.
  4. The sharpest discriminating experiment: in the Slc3a1-knockout model, an evening dose should outperform a morning dose at matched total daily dose, and the margin should widen as half-life shortens. A potency-centred view predicts no time-of-dosing effect; this model predicts one.
  5. The potency requirement should behave as a floor. Compounds spanning roughly 40 nM to 1 µM should perform comparably on a well-timed schedule, diverging only under morning-only dosing with rapid clearance.

6 Limitations

Nucleation parameters are calibrated to plausibility, not fitted to data. The prefactor and barrier were set so that untreated cystinuric urine nucleates on an hour timescale at S≈3 and remains stable over ~100 h at S≈1.5. They are not fitted to a published cystine induction-time series. This is the principal weakness of the model. Varying each by factors of two and ten shifts the headline reduction by only a few percent, which is reassuring but is not a substitute for calibration against measured induction times.

Pharmacokinetic parameters are assumed. Bioavailability, fraction excreted unchanged, half-life and absorption rate were not available and were varied instead. Driving bioavailability to 0.05 and fractional excretion to 0.10 simultaneously changes the modelled reduction from 98.8% to 98.6%, because the potency margin is large; nonetheless these values should be checked against measured data before the conclusions are relied upon.

The weakest result. If the inhibitor acts only on growth and does not raise the nucleation barrier, modelled reduction falls from 98.8% to 81%. This remains substantial but is the assumption most worth attacking.

Structural simplifications. One-compartment pharmacokinetics; bladder urine treated as well mixed; a representative molecular weight assumed for dose conversion. The gene-correction part of the cure rests on one mouse study and small human transplant series; the 51–60% target is arithmetic on this model’s own ratio, not a measured threshold. The model omits crystal aggregation, secondary nucleation on existing stones, Randall’s-plaque anchoring, medullary concentration gradients, urinary macromolecules that act as endogenous inhibitors, and any activity of the mixed-disulfide metabolite.

No safety modelling of any kind: not toxicity, renal accumulation, drug interaction, or chronic exposure. A compound that suppresses crystallization and injures the tubule would be scored as a success by this model, which is a reason to treat its output as a hypothesis-generating instrument only.

Reproducing known clinical facts demonstrates internal consistency. It is a precondition for usefulness and is some distance from establishing that the model is correct. No claim is made here about the probability of clinical success, which would require priors on translational performance of this mechanism class, on the fidelity of the murine model, and on safety, none of which this work addresses.
